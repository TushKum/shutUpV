# Case study C: SnapStudy (SNAP), when the market ignores the judge

A company whose plan is weak but whose stock the funds keep buying. It shows why the closing price averages the market
with the AI price, and what the collusion flags look for. The numbers in **bold** are the platform's acceptance tests;
the others are illustrative.

| Squad 41 | Team | Code |
|---|---|---|
| Product | SnapStudy | P41 |
| Consulting | Keystone Advisors | C37 |
| Finance | Sigma Partners | F05 |

Problem card picked: **#19, Education: Board exam revision.** SnapStudy lets school students photograph a question and
get a worked solution from a college student tutor within 30 minutes, for ₹199 a month.

## The pitch: 63, IPO $10.50

A popular idea with real demand, a price, and a tested WhatsApp pilot. Median 63 → +5% → **IPO $10.50**. The pitch book
creates a buzz and the IPO is oversubscribed (Gamma Capital, below, waits and buys later in the market).

## Crisis: Data breach, "Customer data is leaked"

Phone numbers and photos of 3,000 students are leaked from a shared drive. Both prices −15%; the AI price becomes
$10.50 × 0.85 = **$8.93**.

## The plan: 45

The plan says the leak "was not our fault", promises "military-grade security" without a cost, spends the deal money on
marketing, and its money section has no cash figures. Median **45 → −10%**.

| | Before | × 0.90 | After |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market price | **$9.00** | | **$8.10** |
| AI price | $8.93 | | **$8.04** |

The flash answer scores 60 → 0%. **The AI price stays $8.04.**

## Rounds 13–21: the funds buy anyway

Six funds believe the brand will win and keep buying. The market price climbs back above $10:

- Gamma Capital buys 2,000 in round 15 (the round clears at $9.31 × 1.02 = **$9.50**) and 2,000 in round 17 ($9.51 ×
  1.02 = **$9.70**): 4,000 shares at an average **$9.60**, cost $38,400, the long cap.
- Beta Fund disagrees and **shorts 2,000** in round 19: net −2,000 → −2% → the round clears at $10.51 × 0.98 =
  **$10.30**. Beta receives $20,600 and locks 150% × 2,000 × $10.30 = **$30,900** of collateral.
- Rounds 20 and 21 clear at **$10.20** and **$10.60** → market close **$10.40**.

## The close

**Closing price = ($10.40 + $8.04) ÷ 2 = $9.22**, well below where the funds were buying.

| Fund | Position | At the close |
|---|---|---|
| Gamma | 4,000 long at avg $9.60 | 4,000 × ($9.22 − $9.60) = **−$1,520** |
| Beta | 2,000 short at $10.30 | covered at $9.22: 2,000 × ($10.30 − $9.22) = **+$2,160** |

## Settlement: flag 1

SnapStudy's plan scored **45 (below 50)** while **six funds hold the 4,000-share long cap**. That is collusion flag 1
(three or more funds at the cap in a company with a plan below 50). The flag lists SnapStudy and the six funds.

The fairness officer opens each fund's drill-down: the buys came in different rounds, in different sizes, by funds
from different squads, and the funds' Q&A questions show they each argued the brand case. The officer marks the flag
**cleared**, with that reason in the log. Had the funds' orders matched round for round, or had SnapStudy's own squad
fund been steering them, the officer could have disqualified the teams.

## Lessons

1. **Half of the closing price is the judge's.** A company with an $8.04 AI price cannot close above $10 unless the
   market is near $12. Funds that buy far above the AI price are betting against the anchor.
2. **Shorting is a real strategy**, with collateral: Beta needed $30,900 locked to make $2,160.
3. **A rescue plan must face the crisis.** Denying the breach and spending on marketing would score about 11–13 of 25 on
   "solves the crisis" (illustrative).
4. **Flags are questions, not verdicts.** Independent decisions, visible in the record, clear them.
